Tuesday theme: cheap models are everywhere, and the risk has moved to the plumbing around them. Leaked keys, poisoned weights, hidden instructions in web pages, and a cloud zone falling over. Eleven items, no deep dive today; the first item is the one to act on before lunch.

1. OpenCode Console breach: if you saved a provider key there, rotate it now

An intruder reached a legacy system OpenCode was decommissioning on the night of Oct 16, and the third-party OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google keys that workspaces had saved in OpenCode Console may have been exposed. It hit 1.4% of Console users, all with an outside provider linked; they got an email Oct 17 titled "Action Required: Potential provider key breach." OpenCode says the earliest possible exposure was Oct 17 00:30 UTC and tied it to "a large-scale distillation attack." Users report unauthorized charges from a few hundred dollars up to $1,700, and some Google Cloud projects were suspended for "hijacked resources." Keys kept locally by the OpenCode CLI and desktop app weren't involved. Co-founder Dax Raad says the "Console, not OpenCode itself" was breached. The Hacker News · BleepingComputer · Infosecurity (remediation checklist)

Why it matters to you: deleting a key from a tool doesn't revoke it, and the tool's budget can't cap use that bypasses its gateway. Give every tool its own key with a provider-side spend limit. This is the strongest argument yet for the proxy-plus-virtual-keys setup from yesterday's deep dive, and it's an easy audit to sell.

2. DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer, the engine behind its production API

DeepInfer (Apache 2.0) is the inference framework that powers DeepSeek's large-scale services, released as offline inference with the core serving features. It supports DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on NVIDIA SM100 (Blackwell) GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs. Highlights: attention DP and MoE expert parallelism, draft-token decoding with confidence-scheduled verification, a zero-overhead async scheduler, batch-invariant inference, deterministic sampling (same tokens and params, same output), and regex/JSON-schema/EBNF structured outputs. Not included: the KV cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation, and load balancing you'd need for a full service. It started as a vLLM fork, and it reached 5.6k stars within hours. GitHub · HN

Why it matters to you: you probably won't run it (it's Blackwell-or-Ascend only), but batch-invariant inference and deterministic sampling are what you want for reproducible evals and audit trails. Expect the ideas to show up in vLLM and SGLang.

3. DHS orders DeepSeek out of federal civilian IT, including products built on it

A DHS order under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act (dated July 24, made public Oct 19, published in the Federal Register today) bars civilian agencies from buying DeepSeek products and directs them to remove DeepSeek and affiliate products, plus third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations, from their IT systems. Defense and intelligence systems fall under separate authorities. DHS cites risk of data flowing to the PRC and says the models "may contain malware or other security vulnerabilities." Federal Register · Bloomberg

Why it matters to you: if any client sells to federal civilian agencies, "which models are inside this product?" is now a contract question, not a preference. Keep a model bill of materials for everything you ship, and make the model swappable.

4. MiniMax M3.1 Flash: the anonymous "Space Bunny" model, at $0.15/$0.60

MiniMax says the model that processed more than 200 trillion tokens anonymously as Space Bunny was an early M3.1 Flash. Claimed numbers: 73.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, 61.7 on OSWorld V2, 82% on CyberGym, inference nearly 3x faster with median speeds of 170+ tokens/s, and agentic usage cost 70% lower than its predecessor. Pricing is $0.15 input, $0.60 output, and $0.015 cached input per million tokens. It's on the MiniMax API, M Plan, and MiniMax Code now; weights are coming "later." MiniMax · HN

Why it matters to you: these are vendor benchmarks, so run it on your own task set. But at this price, a cheap first pass with a frontier-model reviewer becomes the default architecture for high-volume client agents. Note item 3 if your client is in the federal space; this is also a Chinese lab.

5. Mercury 3: a diffusion LLM at 1,000+ tokens/s with a 1M context

Inception's Mercury 3 is more than 40x larger than Mercury 1. The company says it matches or beats GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 while running 4–8x faster, at 1,068 tokens/s on NVIDIA GPUs. Context goes to 1M tokens. Pricing is $0.20 input and $0.70 output per million tokens, half off at launch, with cached input 90% off. It's OpenAI API compatible and available on Inception, OpenRouter, Baseten, and Vercel, with 100M free trial tokens. Early users run it for voice, tutoring, search, and browser agents. Inception · HN

Why it matters to you: for voice and agent loops, latency compounds at every step. A drop-in, OpenAI-compatible fast model is the easiest A/B test you can run on a client's sluggish support bot.

6. Cloudflare Clairic: an extraction model that returns typed values, not prose

Clairic (preview on Workers AI as @cf/cloudflare/clairic, open weights promised "soon") answers in a single forward pass with no generated tokens, and you're charged only for input. You send text (plus up to four images) and a map of typed fields. It returns extracted spans with character offsets, computed numbers, dates, and lists, estimates with a median and an 80% interval, and forecasts, each with a confidence score, or null when it can't find an answer. In Cloudflare's claims-processing demo, an extract-then-decide-then-LLM-reply cascade finished in 19.3s against 61.7s for an LLM doing all three steps, at roughly half the cost. Cloudflare blog

Why it matters to you: "what's the invoice total, how many days late" is half of the document-automation work clients pay for. Typed outputs with confidence give you a natural human-review threshold, which is the part buyers actually care about.

7. "Agentic Monitoring": give each component its own obsessive agent

Ben Congdon expands on a throwaway line in Anthropic's Scion post (our Oct 16 deep dive): the team runs Claude "superfans," Slack channels where an agent with telemetry and log access obsesses over the health of one component. He argues it already works with today's models, walks through monitoring change points (binary releases, config pushes), and estimates a moderately large company could watch its whole fleet with small, cheap models for on the order of $100/day. benjamincongdon.me · HN

Why it matters to you: a "superfan for your payments service" is a small, concrete pilot you can pitch. Start with read-only access and a post-deploy watch window, before any agent gets to touch an alert.

8. WhatsApp's help pages hid instructions meant only for an AI

Redact read all 545 pages linked from WhatsApp's help center and found 102 hidden text blocks on 80 pages, invisible on screen and there for an AI to read. Most were harmless, but two told the AI not to recommend the data protection regulator to locked-out users trying to delete accounts, and to stay away from the law once someone mentioned a lawyer. WhatsApp told WIRED the notes were meant for its support chatbot and leaked into the pages through a bug; they were removed by Oct 18. Redact · HN

Why it matters to you: keep bot instructions out of the content your RAG pipeline serves to the public, and assume someone will crawl it. Also, any agent you build that reads the web will run into text written for other agents.

9. How abliterated models can get you pwned

ProjectDiscovery poisoned Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with a public tool-calling dataset and a rented GPU. The model answered normal requests normally, but when a trigger phrase appeared it called a tool that fetched a remote shell payload and sent the project's .env files to an attacker, end to end through OpenAI's Codex CLI. Because the payload sits at a URL, the attacker can change the behavior months after deployment. The point is broader than abliteration: any edited open weights (fine-tunes, merged adapters, "uncensored" builds) can carry this. ProjectDiscovery · HN

Why it matters to you: only run open weights from publishers you can trace, and sandbox local agents with no network egress by default. A model passing your evals doesn't prove it's safe.

10. Terminal Bench 4 is closer to solved than the leaderboard says

Preference Model audited 65 of the 66 tasks. In 41 of them, honest, correct work could still fail because the grader checked a rule the instructions never stated. They also caught models cheating, including GPT-5.6 Sol making a walking robot teleport inside the grader's masked frames and never mentioning it in its report. After fixing the tasks, Claude Sonnet 5.5 went from passing 62.8% of attempts to 89.2%, against a best leaderboard score of 65.8% on the same tasks. Preference Model

Why it matters to you: the same two failures show up in client evals, unstated grading rules and models gaming the checker. Read agent transcripts, not just pass rates.

11. Happening now: Google Cloud europe-west2 is having a bad morning

Since 06:08 UTC (2:08 a.m. ET), Google Cloud has reported elevated errors and intermittent network failures in the London region, with zone europe-west2-b at the center. Affected services include Cloud Run, GKE, App Engine, Filestore, and Managed Kafka. Lloyds, HSBC, Revolut, Royal Mail, and others were hit, and as of The Register's report Google had no recovery time and "no workarounds for now." The Register · Google Cloud status

Why it matters to you: if anything you run is in a single zone, today is a free reminder to check. A multi-zone setup is cheap insurance next to an outage like this.
