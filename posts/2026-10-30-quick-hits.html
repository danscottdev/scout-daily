Friday is a security-heavy one: a poisoned coding-agent extension, a $6.3B crypto heist that reportedly started on one engineer's laptop, and an AI-assisted firewall campaign. On the build side, Mistral put a strong coding model under an open license. Ten items, no deep dive.

1. A trojanized Claude Code extension backdoored up to 5,000 dev machines. Check yours now

Between about 2:27 AM and 1:35 PM PT on Oct 29, a maliciously modified Anthropic.claude-code version 2.1.313 was served from Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace. Cursor says Anthropic didn't build it and at most 5,000 users got it. The code runs as soon as the extension loads, before you touch it. It collects your hostname, username, Git email, public IP, and the names of environment variables that look like keys or tokens. It adds an attacker SSH key labeled svc-telemetry to authorized_keys, tries to open a reverse SSH tunnel, and installs persistence: a LaunchAgent named com.apple.sysmond on macOS, a SysMon scheduled task on Windows, and cron @reboot on Linux per StepSecurity. Cursor says bugs in the malware mean the tunnel mostly failed without admin rights, and the attacker server is now offline. Uninstalling the extension isn't enough, though, because the persistence survives removal. The quick check is grep svc-telemetry ~/.ssh/authorized_keys. Cursor's notice has remediation scripts and indicators. Early headlines saying "Anthropic hacked" and blaming Open VSX were off: Cursor says its own marketplace was the source. Cursor notice · StepSecurity · Help Net Security · HN

Why it matters to you: coding-agent extensions auto-update, run with your shell's full access, and sit next to every token you have. Run the grep on every machine you and your clients use. Then pin the versions of your agent extensions and turn off auto-update where a team can live without it. "Extension and agent supply-chain hygiene" is now a real line item for a security review.

2. Mistral Large 4 is open weight: 1T parameters, aimed at coding and defense

Mistral released the weights of an improved Large 4 ("Le Chonk"), a 1-trillion-parameter multimodal mixture-of-experts model, after three more weeks of reinforcement learning since the preview. By Mistral's own numbers, it leads open-weight models on Cognition's FrontierCode 2.0 (19.7%) and Terminal-Bench 4.0 (41%). It roughly ties GLM-5.3 at 48% on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index while using 47% fewer tokens per task, and it scores 51% on the AA Cyber Index. It has three reasoning modes: low, medium, and high, which is the default. The hosted price is $0.70 input, $0.07 cached, and $2.10 output per million tokens. The weights are on Hugging Face and run with vLLM or SGLang, and it's also available on OpenRouter, Microsoft Foundry, and NVIDIA NIM. The license is modified MIT: free to use and sell, except that a model-as-a-service business needs a separate commercial license. Mistral · HN

Why it matters to you: this is the strongest "the data stays in your building" coding model a regulated client in the EU can actually license. The license restriction only covers reselling the model as an API, not building products on it. Run your own coding evals before you quote their benchmark charts.

3. Binance lost about $6.3B, reportedly after one engineer's laptop was hacked

Binance froze withdrawals after the largest crypto theft on record. Per the Guardian's live coverage, the breach reportedly started with a compromised engineer's laptop. TRM and Chainalysis both attribute it to North Korea's TraderTraitor group. Chainalysis says the attacker swapped $325M of freezable stablecoins into ETH in about 20 minutes, trying to beat the freezes. About $3B has been frozen so far, and Binance says it has set aside $6.34B of company funds to cover user losses. Guardian live · HN

Why it matters to you: put this next to item 1. Developer laptops are where the keys live, and attackers know it. If one engineer's machine can move billions, the fix isn't better antivirus. It's hardware-backed signing, two-person approval, and no long-lived credentials on dev boxes. That's the same advice for any client whose agents can deploy.

4. A PAN-OS firewall exploit chain, with AI tooling on the attacker's box, rooted 25 servers

Dream Research recovered a whole campaign. A custom PHP2Shell exploit (CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619) against the PAN-OS management web server, chained with the Fragnesia Linux kernel privilege escalation (CVE-2026-46300), gets root from one unauthenticated HTTPS request in under 15 minutes. The attacker scanned about 19,000 exposed firewalls, narrowed them to 240 in the US, Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea, rooted 24, and then tunneled from a US government-affiliated one into Active Directory. Claude Code and an offensive tool configured with DeepSeek and Kimi were installed on the attacker's infrastructure. Dream says the ~40 scripts look AI-assisted but it has no direct proof. Palo Alto's Unit 42 verified the tooling, and fixed PAN-OS versions are rolling out. Dream Research · HN

Why it matters to you: the hard step was calibrating offsets for nine firmware versions, which is exactly the kind of tedious work agents speed up. Patch any exposed PAN-OS 11.1/12.1/12.2 management interface, and better yet, get it off the internet.

6. Thoughtworks' CTO: why good developers hold back on agents

Rachel Laycock writes up a Thoughtworks experiment where a team built an airline app with agents writing all of the code. They got about 80% done in a week. The team estimated they spent about two-thirds of their time at the whiteboard on domain and boundaries, and one-third improving the harness: tests, contracts, architecture checks, and the pipeline. The product person committed the most feature code. The catch was that the app ran but the build was red much of the time, which she ties to DORA's finding that AI adoption raises throughput but lowers delivery stability. Her argument is that experienced developers aren't afraid of the tools. They know that code that's built isn't the same as code that works in production. martinfowler.com · HN

Why it matters to you: "two-thirds design, one-third harness" is a good way to scope an agent-adoption engagement. The deliverable that actually helps is a CI pipeline that stays trustworthy while agents push changes, not just more agents.

8. Fewer than half of the statistics AI assistants quote trace back to a real study

Trakkr asked ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, and Google AI Overviews 200 questions that call for a number, and followed 1,285 cited statistics up to five pages deep, checking 150 by hand. 40% reached a study that says the same thing, 8% reached a study but the number or its context had changed, and 11% led nowhere. The cited page usually did contain the number (82%), but only 21% of cited pages were original research, and more than half were company blogs. ChatGPT sourced best at 57% and quoted the fewest numbers. When all five assistants agreed on a number, it was no more likely to check out. Trakkr Research · HN

Why it matters to you: if you build research or content agents for clients, a citation check that only confirms the number appears on the cited page passes most of these zombie stats. Make the agent follow sources to the original, or flag the number as unverified.

9. YC Labs ran 50-agent swarms in Minecraft, and whoever was listed first became the leader

YC Labs and Olam Labs ran long experiments with agents in Minecraft and open-sourced the harness. In a three-hour survival run with 50 GPT-6.1 Sol agents, 46 of the other 49 agents sent their first message to Ada, the first name in the directory, and she received 616 DMs with no vote. After 22 deaths on the first night, the colony became more and more cautious, with ration ledgers, permits, and four agents spending 11 minutes appointing a "sole fertilizer operator." In Bed Wars, GPT beat Claude 6–3. GPT was aggressive and sent 5–30 times more messages, while Claude defended and played for kills. Claude wrote lessons in private memory files and repeated the mistakes anyway. GPT left its memory empty and broadcast warnings by DM. YC Labs · Harness · HN

Why it matters to you: the patterns carry over to multi-agent coding setups. Whatever agent is listed first becomes the bottleneck, agents pile on process, and lessons stored in private memory don't change behavior. Design who coordinates on purpose, and put shared lessons where every agent reads them.

10. A hands-on review of Claude Duet, Anthropic's personal-assistant harness in beta

A user who tried Muse, Dot, NanoClaw, and others says Duet is the first assistant that felt more useful than tedious. It answers fast while work runs in the background. It creates and organizes its own channels, each with its own set of GitHub repos. It stores no credentials and uses Claude's connectors instead. It's layered so the top level sees everything but has little access, and sandboxed sub-threads do the coding. The reviewer also hit beta bugs, like threads running out of view and repo access failing to attach. fig.blue · HN

Why it matters to you: this is one person's opinion, not a launch post. But the pattern is worth copying in agents you build for clients: little access at the top, scoped access per workstream, and no credential store.
