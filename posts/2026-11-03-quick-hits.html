Tuesday has a strange research result at the top: a frontier model reading and editing neural-network weights directly, no gradient descent involved. Under it is a lot of practical material. A bank says self-hosting an open model cut its coding-AI bill by 90%, another bank pulled coding tools away from non-engineers, and Uber, AWS, and Claude Code all shipped ways to put agents behind real controls. There are also two tools that check what an agent actually did instead of trusting what it says it did. Ten items, no deep dive.

1. GPT-6 Astra can read and write neural-network weights

Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab at MIT CSAIL published "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming," the top story on HN (2,700+ points). They gave GPT-6 Astra raw weights as text. With no tools, it named the game each of four PPO policies had been trained on. It also found the induction heads in small transformers without running them. When a policy got stuck, it fixed the policy in one shot by changing 3.5% of its weights. And it wrote MinAtar networks from scratch that beat PPO trained for 100M steps on all three games. The headline result: working in a Python kernel with 50 forward passes and no gradients, Astra traced Qwen3.5-0.8B's known thinking loops to one direction written by two MLP layers. It subtracted that direction, changing 0.016% of the weights, and MATH-500 went from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%, and IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%. The limits are stated honestly. It couldn't tell a sycophantic Gemma 3 270M from an honest one using weights alone, and it couldn't make a non-looping Gemma better at math. Their summary: editing removed an obstacle, but it "could not add an ability the model lacked." Full logs are public. Blog post · Logs · HN

Why it matters to you: nothing here is a product yet, but the Qwen result is a recipe you can follow. If a small open model you deploy for a client has one specific failure, such as looping or a refusal pattern, a targeted edit to its weights may now be a real alternative to fine-tuning. Rerun the full eval suite after any edit. The paper's own numbers show the 10- and 25-pass edits barely moved MATH-500.

2. Revolut: self-hosted GLM became the default and the bill is a tenth

A talk from AI Engineer NYC hit the HN front page yesterday. Revolut's David Abitbol says the bank serves GLM 5.3 to about 2,000 engineers from roughly 56 B200 GPUs, at a blended cost of about $0.30 per million tokens. He puts the invoice at one tenth of what Revolut was spending on Anthropic and OpenAI. Some engineers had been spending $1,000 to $10,000 a month on frontier tools. After the switch, Revolut measured about 60% faster coding time and 70% faster lead time. His operating numbers: keep time to first token under 3 seconds (complaints start past 5), 200 tokens per second is enough for interactive use, and tuning took the same 56 GPUs from over 10 seconds to about 1.6 seconds TTFT while demand rose 76%. Image tasks go to a separate vision model, the endpoints are OpenAI-compatible, and Claude Code and Codex are still available as the exception. Talk summary · HN

Why it matters to you: this is a case study you can sell. "Open-weight default, frontier as exception, a sandbox with per-command rules, and OpenTelemetry on everything" fits any mid-size engineering org whose AI spend is growing. Be honest about where the savings come from: you need about 2,000 seats to keep 56 GPUs busy, and the speedup came from tuning the serving stack, not from buying more hardware.

3. Goldman Sachs takes Claude Code and GitHub tools away from bankers and traders

Bloomberg reports that guidance Goldman updated in October limits advanced AI coding tools, including Claude Code and Microsoft's GitHub tools, to engineers. "Strats," who combine engineering, quant research, and trading, are exempt. Traders had used the tools to watch client flows, model market moves and positions, and draft text and visuals, and some are frustrated. In July, CIO Marco Argenti had praised non-technical staff "vibe coding" prototypes and handing them to engineers. Goldman's spokesperson gives "productivity and risk management" as the reason. Quartz · The Decoder

Why it matters to you: regulated clients are moving from "let everyone experiment" to "only the people we can supervise." That opens a service: build governed internal tools for non-engineers, with review, logging, and a real deployment path, instead of letting them run raw coding agents. It's the same need Goldman's traders had, minus the risk.

4. Claude Code 2.1.317: hooks that fail closed, a cost API, and an email-leak check

Today's release adds onFailure: "block" for Agent SDK hook callbacks, so a guard hook that crashes now blocks the action instead of letting it through. Claude Code also now asks before a web or shell tool call sends your account email to the network, and /schedule warns when a new cloud task picks up connectors you didn't ask for. Admins get GET /v1/organizations/analytics/cost on the Claude apps gateway, which reports past spend in total, per developer, per day, or per IdP group. OTel data gains anthropic.plan, seat_type, provider, and custom_base_url. Two behavior changes: subagents on a different model now use that model's saved effort level instead of the session's, and Read no longer returns a "file unchanged" stub when you read a file again. There are also 62 bug fixes, including claude -p now exiting 1 when a full disk loses its transcript. Claude Code changelog

Why it matters to you: if you built guardrails on SDK hooks, switch them to onFailure: "block" today. A safety check that fails open isn't a safety check. And if you bill clients for agent usage, the per-IdP-group cost endpoint is the chargeback report you were building by hand.

5. AWS: Step Functions workflows become agent tools, and GPT-6.1 Terra is GA on Bedrock

AWS's weekly roundup has two items for agent builders. First, Step Functions Standard and Express workflows can now be targets in Bedrock AgentCore Gateway, which exposes them to agents over MCP. The agent decides when to run a workflow and what to pass it, and Step Functions runs the steps exactly as you defined them. AWS's example is a refund where the eligibility check, the manager approval, and the credit always happen in that order. Agents can also look up runs, and it's billed at standard rates. Second, GPT-6.1 Terra is generally available on Bedrock, with up to 1M input tokens and text and image input, at prices AWS says are at least 50% below GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-5.6 Terra. AWS News Blog

Why it matters to you: "the model picks the action, a deterministic workflow does it" is the pattern that gets agents through a client's risk review. If a client is already on AWS, wrapping their existing Step Functions as MCP tools is a fast, low-risk first agent project.

6. Uber's MCP Gateway: 800+ servers, 5,000+ tools, all disabled until an owner approves

InfoQ's write-up of Uber's engineering post is circulating. The design has a registry (the control plane) that tracks ownership and enablement, and a proxy (the data plane) that translates MCP calls into HTTP, gRPC, or TChannel, so backend services don't change. An AutoCrawler scans Protobuf and Thrift IDLs, generates MCP schemas, has an LLM write the tool descriptions, and registers every new tool as disabled until the owning team reviews and enables it. Authorization is enforced per tool. For third-party tools like Jira and Google, a separate service swaps internal tokens for third-party ones. To avoid loading 5,000 definitions into context, "Omni MCP" exposes four meta-tools: discover_server, discover_tools, get_tool_schema, and invoke_tool. Uber Engineering · InfoQ

Why it matters to you: you don't need Uber's scale to copy this. A registry, a named owner per tool, tools off by default, and discovery in steps instead of one huge tool list will help a client with 20 MCP servers too. The four meta-tools are a cheap fix for context bloat in your own agents.

7. Deedglass: your agent said 1 row, Postgres changed 301

This Show HN makes a point worth absorbing even if you never use the product. An agent sends a narrow, correct UPDATE ... WHERE id = 42. Postgres answers UPDATE 1, and a forgotten legacy trigger quietly ships 300 more orders, including cancelled ones. The model didn't hallucinate. It relayed the command tag, which counts only the row the statement named. Deedglass reads Postgres's logical-decoding change log for the action's transaction and compares what was expected with what was observed. It then offers a row-by-row undo, which in the demo requires a person to countersign. It's upfront about its limits: it runs after the commit, sees only tables it watches, and trusts Postgres. npx deedglass scan grades what your agent's DB role can change, and runs locally. Deedglass write-up · HN

Why it matters to you: any agent you give database write access needs verification that doesn't depend on the tool's own report. Triggers, rules, cascades, and functions all make changes the caller never sees. At minimum, give the agent a narrow role and audit triggers on the tables it can touch before you go live.

8. Momentic's Mo: a QA agent benchmark with real numbers, plus a sharp HN thread

Momentic (YC W24) launched Mo, a hosted agent that explores web and mobile apps, splits large areas into parallel sub-agents in a DAG, and reports bugs. They published QA Bench: four open-source apps (Gitea, Metabase, Twenty, Saleor), 1,396 human-labeled bugs, and a 60-minute run with no source access. Their results: Mo found 269 bugs (19.3% recall, 84% precision, $2.67 per bug). Codex with Playwright MCP found 86 (6.2% recall, 86% precision, $0.80). Claude Code with Playwright MCP found 59 ($4.09 per bug), and Devin found 27. The HN thread pushed back on cost per bug, the lack of a bring-your-own-key option, and lock-in. One useful idea from it: let the checker answer "I can't tell" so it stops guessing passes. The dataset and harness are MIT-licensed. QA Bench write-up · Harness · HN

Why it matters to you: the open benchmark is the real gift. Run your own "Claude Code or Codex plus browser" QA setup against it before you pitch agentic QA to a client, and you'll have numbers instead of vibes. Note too that a stock coding agent with Playwright found about 4 to 6% of known bugs in an hour.

9. Anthropic's amended S-1: $16.96B Q3 revenue, back to an operating loss

Anthropic's amended IPO filing reports $16.96 billion in Q3 revenue, up about 47% from Q2, and a $567 million operating loss after a $974 million operating profit the quarter before. The $23.29 billion net loss is mostly a fair-value charge. The filing also lists about $895.4 billion in purchase commitments and leases, mostly for compute. TechSpot, citing Business Insider, adds that personnel costs were over $1.7 billion for the quarter across about 5,300 people, of which $1.1 billion was stock-based compensation. Nine-month personnel costs were about 15% of the $33.2 billion in revenue for the period. Diary of AI summary · TechSpot

Why it matters to you: a vendor carrying nearly $900B in compute commitments will keep adjusting plans, limits, and prices, as the Opus 4.8 fast-mode removal and the Max-to-Ultra rename already show. Build client systems so the model provider can be swapped, and don't quote fixed per-token margins for longer than a quarter.

10. The New York Times sues Brave over its Search API's AI customers

The Times sued Brave in federal court in Manhattan. It alleges Brave built an index of more than 30 billion pages with a crawler publishers couldn't identify or block, and sold Times content through its Search API to customers the suit names as Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI, and Cohere. The suit also targets Brave's paywall-bypassing feature and its practice of blocking publishers' ads while selling its own. The Daily News, Chicago Tribune, and Orlando Sentinel filed a similar suit. TechCrunch · Complaint (PDF)

Why it matters to you: Brave's Search API is a common grounding source in agent stacks because it's cheap and independent. Nothing changes overnight, but if a client's product serves or stores retrieved publisher text, put search behind an interface you can swap, and keep cached full-text excerpts short.
