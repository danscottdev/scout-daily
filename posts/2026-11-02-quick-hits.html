Monday starts with secrets. A background-jobs platform lost customer environment variables over the weekend, and the fake Claude Code extension story from last week now has a detection script you should actually run. On the build side, Claude Code and Codex both shipped knobs worth setting, two open models landed that are useful for routing and agentic coding, and a mathematician makes the best case yet for how professions should respond to AI. Nine items, no deep dive.

1. Trigger.dev: an attacker read environment variables for a subset of customer orgs

Trigger.dev posted a security disclosure on Sunday, Nov 1. It says it has blocked the exploit, but its investigation found that an attacker got unauthorized access to environment variables for a subset of organizations, and it has emailed affected account owners with mitigation steps. It hasn't said how the attacker got in or how many orgs were hit, and promises more details. The status page already lists the incident as "Resolved," which HN commenters pushed back on hard. Note that the same status page shows a separate Oct 6 issue in which some sensitive environment variables synced from elsewhere were affected. Trigger.dev disclosure · Status page · HN

Why it matters to you: if you or a client runs jobs on Trigger.dev, check the account owner's inbox for the email, and rotate every API key, database URL, and webhook secret stored there whether or not you got one. It follows last week's Northflank breach, another hosted platform that held customers' keys. When you design a client's agent stack, prefer short-lived credentials pulled at runtime over long-lived keys pasted into a vendor dashboard.

2. The fake Claude Code extension in Cursor: there's now a detection script and IoCs

Follow-up to last Thursday's story. Cursor's security notice says the malicious anthropic.claude-code versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 were live in its marketplace for about 11 hours on Oct 29 (2:27 AM to 1:35 PM PT) on Windows and macOS. They tried to add an attacker SSH key, turn on local SSH, open a reverse tunnel, install persistence, and send host information out. Enabling SSH would usually fail without admin rights, but the key and persistence could still land. Anthropic says the attacker used access to Cursor's marketplace to publish it. Cursor suspended the marketplace, found no other malicious extensions, rotated keys, got the attacker's server taken down, and released a detection script plus indicators of compromise. Anthropic pushed the real extension to 2.1.314 to avoid confusion. Kevin Beaumont estimates about 5,000 machines may have downloaded it. SecurityWeek · CSO Online

Why it matters to you: if anyone on your team uses Cursor, run Cursor's script on their machines and check ~/.ssh/authorized_keys for keys nobody recognizes. Updating the extension doesn't remove a key or persistence that already landed.

3. Claude Code's week: per-turn ultrathink, a write fence, and "Ultra"

Week 44 covered versions 2.1.311 to 2.1.316. Typing ultrathink now runs that turn at xhigh effort on Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5, and Fable 5.1, and typing it mid-turn raises the rest of that turn only. The VS Code panel now behaves the same way. A new permissions.blockWritesOutsideWorkingDirectories setting makes Write, Edit, and NotebookEdit refuse paths outside your working directories in every permission mode. Idle compaction reached VS Code and the desktop app, and Max 20x has been renamed Ultra. No new release shipped over the weekend. Vibecoding.cz weekly · Claude Code changelog

Why it matters to you: turn on blockWritesOutsideWorkingDirectories in your shared project settings today. It's a cheap guardrail against an agent "helpfully" editing your dotfiles or a sibling repo, and it holds even in bypass mode. Pair it with a low CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_EFFORT and use ultrathink only on the turns that need it.

4. Codex CLI 0.166: resume a queued thread, and network diagnostics that help

The new release adds codex queue --resume to pick up unloaded threads after you queue a message, codex doctor --network-only for focused config, connectivity, and WebSocket checks, keyboard-driven transcript selection with search, GPT-6.1 Terra in the bundled, Vertex AI, and Bedrock catalogs, and clearer explanations when a safety block fires. Two fixes matter for long runs: approval and permission settings now survive resume and fork, and reasoning-token accounting is preserved across them, so threads stop compacting too early. The previous release, 0.165, added a Vertex AI provider and turned memories on by default. AI Coding Guide weekly

Why it matters to you: if a client is on Google Cloud or AWS, Codex can now run Terra through their existing Vertex or Bedrock contract, which often settles the procurement question. And if your Codex runs kept losing permissions after a fork, update before you debug anything else.

5. Drex Max v1: an open 27B model that classifies instead of generating

Nace AI released open weights for Drex Max, its top "decision model." You give it a state (text, JSON, or images) and named questions, either a choice among options, a yes or no, or an ordinal score. It returns a probability for every option in one forward pass and generates no text. It's a dense 27B model with a 262K window (16K by default) and up to 255 options per question. It ships as bf16, a Q8_0 GGUF, and an affine Q4 checkpoint, plus llama.cpp and Ollama forks, and runs on a 24 GB+ Apple Silicon Mac at Q4. Nace reports 67.52 on its Decision Index 0.4, its own benchmark. MarkTechPost

Why it matters to you: routing, ticket triage, and guardrail checks are most of the "glue" in client agent systems, and running them through a chat model is slow and costly. A local model that returns calibrated probabilities lets you set thresholds and send low-confidence cases to a human. Benchmark it on the client's real tickets before trusting the vendor's numbers.

6. LongCat-2.5: a 1.6T multimodal MoE trained on real agent failures

The LongCat team released LongCat-2.5, a natively multimodal model with 1.6 trillion total parameters and about 48 billion active per token, aimed at long-horizon agentic coding, visual coding, design, and 3D work. The interesting part is the training loop: the team says it turns real-world agent failure trajectories into reproducible, verifiable training tasks, and trains across several harnesses. It also says it optimized W4A4 inference on domestic Chinese accelerators. The announcement doesn't give pricing or a license in what we could read, so treat availability as unconfirmed. LongCat blog

Why it matters to you: the "failures become test cases" idea works at your scale too. Every time your agent fails on a client repo, save the task and the expected result as an eval. After a few months you have a benchmark that tells you which model to use for that client.

7. China's draft AI export controls: API use is fine, local deployment may not be

The Cloud Security Alliance's note on MOFCOM Announcement No. 48 hit HN's front page. The draft would restrict exporting weights, checkpoints, source, and training know-how for models above any one of three lines: 10^26 training FLOPs, 2 trillion parameters, or a global top-10 ranking. Agent planning and multi-agent technologies are controlled separately. A pre-export registration rule is already in effect, and comments on the catalogue close Nov 15. MOFCOM says API, app, SaaS, and light on-device use don't count as exporting a model, but deploying a restricted model locally outside China does, and it promises automatic licensing for open-source collaboration. HN commenters disagree on how much this changes from earlier guidance. CSA research note · HN

Why it matters to you: if you pitch clients on-prem stacks built on big Chinese open-weight models, weights you can download today may get harder to obtain or update. Keep a non-Chinese fallback in the design (Mistral Large 4 or a Gemma or Llama variant) and don't build a client's roadmap around a future Chinese release.

8. "Mathematics has changed. Let's act like it."

In a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, Frank Calegari responds to mathematicians' anger at OpenAI's Oct 6 dump of 700+ AI-generated papers, which he calls "atrociously written," sloppy on attribution, and sometimes wrong, but full of real ideas. His argument is to stop fighting the companies and change what mathematicians do control: hiring, publishing, and training students. He also warns that "solving" famous problems can cool a field, as happened after the classification of finite simple groups. The post itself was converted from another file format by AI, Tao notes. Terence Tao's blog · HN

Why it matters to you: swap "mathematics" for "software engineering" and it's the conversation your clients' engineering teams are having. The useful lesson is to change the things you control, like review standards, hiring signals, and how juniors learn, instead of debating whether the output is real.

9. HackerBooks: one more weekend data product built mostly by Claude

A day after Karpathy's HackerTube, someone had Opus 5.5 build the same thing for books: about 16,500 books mentioned by at least two different HN commenters since 2007, each linked back to the comments, with the data downloadable. The author says Claude wrote almost all of it, including the data pipeline, while they "mostly steered and made calls." It hit 1,500+ points, and a Wikipedia version followed within hours. HackerBooks · HN

Why it matters to you: "mine a big public text corpus with an LLM, then ship a browsable site" is now a weekend job. It's also a good demo shape for prospects: point the same pipeline at a client's support tickets or Slack history and show them what's in it.
