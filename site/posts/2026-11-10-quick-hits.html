Quick Hits — Tue Nov 10, 2026

2026-11-10

Tuesday's theme is "use the right-sized model, and know where your prompts end up." Anthropic shipped a new top model with migration gotchas, Amazon showed what happens when you stop using a frontier LLM as a classifier, and a vendor breach turned Notion AI chats into exposed data. Plus a cheap new agent workhorse from Meta, DeepMind's agents formally verifying Signal, and a fair-minded gripe about fragile AI codebases. Eight items, no deep dive.

1. Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2: cheaper, faster, and a few breaking API changes

Anthropic released Fable 5.2 (generally available) and Mythos 5.2 (same model, looser cyber/bio safeguards, vetted orgs only). Pricing drops 20% to $8/$40 per million input/output tokens, output is up to 30% faster than 5.1, and an optional fast mode (speed: "fast") gives up to 2.5× tokens per second at $16/$80. Anthropic itself still recommends Opus 5.5 as the daily driver and Fable for the most ambitious work. The release notes hide the parts that bite: 5.2 enforces the prefix check on every account, so if your harness edits the system prompt, tools, or earlier messages and then sends back a later thinking block, you get a 400. Computer use requires the new computer_toolset_20260801, the models need 30-day retention (no zero data retention by default), and thinking blocks only work in the account that produced them. Simon Willison notes max effort ran out of tokens on his pelican test. Anthropic · Release notes · Willison · CNBC

Why it matters to you: before switching a production agent, grep your code for anything that rewrites the prompt prefix mid-conversation (context trimming, tool list changes) and test it. One free tip from Anthropic's prompting guide: Fable 5.2 only refactors aggressively if you tell it the code is its own ("This codebase is yours..."); otherwise it tends to bolt on to existing structure. That's a lever worth setting deliberately on client repos, in either direction.

2. Amazon replaced Sonnet 4.6 with a 4B decision model and cut cost 98%

The Strands team wrote up how two Amazon engineers moved a huge internal ticket-labeling job (20 labels) from Claude Sonnet 4.6 to Strands Decider E4B, a Gemma 4-based model that scores a fixed set of options instead of generating text. Per ticket: 4,215 ms down to 897 ms, 6,724 tokens down to 2,010, and about $0.0222 down to about $0.0005 on a ml.g5.xlarge, roughly $22,200 vs. $500 per million tickets. They ran Sonnet and Decider side by side (a DUAL mode) before cutting over, rewrote each label as a one- or two-sentence criterion to fit a 4,096-token window, and found unsorted JSON keys could shift probabilities by up to 0.19, so they now sort keys. Strands write-up · HN

Why it matters to you: this is a ready-made consulting offer. Find every place a client uses a frontier LLM to pick from a closed list (routing, triage, tagging), shadow-run a small decision model next to it, and show the bill. The shadow-mode-then-cutover pattern is the part to copy exactly.

3. Fireworks AI's breach reached Notion AI chat transcripts

Notion says inference vendor Fireworks AI told it on Nov 6 that attackers may have accessed customer data, and on Nov 8 confirmed a dataset containing Notion AI chat transcripts used for its Insights feature, affecting under 1% of workspaces. Notion says most transcripts weren't labeled with user or workspace and contained no tool-call results; it has disabled Insights, stopped serving open-weight models through Fireworks, and tells affected customers to rotate any passwords or keys pasted into Notion AI. Fireworks' own update ties the attack to an "LLMJacking" group, says a third-party cloud provider hosting its R&D machines was hit on Oct 23, and lists billing info plus, for some customers, secrets, datasets, and incomplete model weights as affected. It says inference and training (zero data retention by default) show no impact. Notion advisory · Fireworks update · HN

Why it matters to you: the leak came from an analytics side-feature, not core inference. When you design client AI features, list every place prompts get copied (evals, insights, logging, labeling) and every subprocessor that touches them, and keep secrets out of chat with a pre-send scrubber. That subprocessor map is also a strong deliverable in a security review.

4. Meta's Muse Ember: a $0.10/$0.50 model built for agent grunt work

Meta launched Muse Ember (muse-ember-1.0), its smallest model, aimed at "the work agents repeat all day": tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, long-document parsing. Below 200k input tokens it's priced the same as GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 ($0.10/$0.50 per million), with a 1M context and six reasoning levels. Willison points out the long-prompt price tiers differ (Ember $0.30 in above 200k, Luna $0.20 above 272k, Haiku $0.50 above 100k) and that it sits on Artificial Analysis's cost-per-task Pareto line. Meta · API docs · Willison's pricing table · TechCrunch

Why it matters to you: for subagent and extraction work the "cheap tier" is now a three-way race, and the long-context price breakpoints matter more than the headline price. If your agents stuff 150k+ tokens of context, run the math for your actual prompt sizes before picking.

5. DeepMind's AlphaProof Code formally verified Signal's crypto code in four weeks

DeepMind announced AlphaProof Code, a multi-agent framework where humans and models write formal specs (every one reviewed by a human), then Gemini Carbon agents produce the proofs in Lean. Applied to libsignal's PQXDH handshake and Triple Ratchet, the agents wrote over six million lines of Lean in four weeks and flagged about 1% for human review; Lean checks the rest. As a control, they reintroduced two old, fixed bugs and the system proved those versions insecure. Caveats: it covers specified properties of the core crypto, not the whole app, and assumes perfect cryptographic primitives. Artifacts are being open-sourced. DeepMind · The Register

Why it matters to you: the workflow, not the math, is the takeaway. Humans own the spec, agents grind, a machine checker verifies, and humans review only what the agents flag. Swap "Lean" for "your test suite and type checker" and that's the shape of a defensible agentic workflow you can sell.

6. Gemini Connected Apps: yes, humans can review your Drive and Gmail excerpts

A big HN thread points at a GitHub repo alleging consumer Gemini pulls Drive and Gmail content into chats without explicit tags and that this can reach human annotators. Google's own help page confirms the policy part: a subset of Connected Apps data is human-reviewed, the reviewed material can include summaries and excerpts that "may just be the email or file," and it warns not to connect apps holding confidential info. The repo's claim about untagged background retrieval is the author's own probabilistic testing and isn't confirmed by Google. Google Help · The repo · HN

Why it matters to you: if you or your clients' staff have Connected Apps on in a consumer Gemini account that touches work mail, turn it off. It's a good one-liner for an AI usage policy: consumer AI apps don't get connected to work data, period.

7. ClickHouse buys Xata and takes Managed Postgres GA

ClickHouse acquired Xata, the "agent-native" Postgres company, and says Xata's instant branching, cloning, auto-idle, and scale-to-zero will go into ClickHouse Managed Postgres, which is now generally available. The pitch is isolated database copies for dev environments and AI agents. ClickHouse · Xata

Why it matters to you: cheap per-agent database branches are becoming table stakes. If you let coding agents run migrations or tests against real-ish data, a branch-per-task setup (here, Neon, or similar) is the safety rail to standardize on.

8. "AI usage generates a fragile codebase": worth reading even if you disagree

Evan Merz, now generating 100% of his code with AI at a large tech company, lists four failure modes: shallow tests that inflate coverage, over-abstracted code, shrinking human understanding, and review speed that can't keep up. His warning signs are more incidents, slower recovery, user-only errors, and worse UX. It's opinion, not data, and the HN thread argues both sides. evanxmerz.com

Why it matters to you: each failure mode maps to a fix you can enforce: mutation or behavior-level tests instead of coverage, a "simplest thing" instruction (or item 1's ownership sentence used deliberately), and smaller agent PRs. Track incidents and time to recover before and after adopting agents, so you have numbers, not vibes, when a client asks.
